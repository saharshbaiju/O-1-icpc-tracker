Project Title: O(1) — an ICPC preparation tracker for competitive-programming teams and their mentors

3.1. Detailed description of the project

Competitive-programming teams preparing for the ICPC train across several websites at once: they solve problems on Codeforces, follow topic ladders a coach hands out, run past regional problem sets as timed practice, and keep a spreadsheet of who has solved what. Nothing joins these together, so a mentor cannot see at a glance which student is behind on which topic, and a student cannot see what to work on next. O(1) is a web application that gives a college's ICPC teams one place to practise, be assigned work by a mentor, and measure progress against each other.

Users

The system has two kinds of user, and a person holds exactly one role:

  • Student: a contestant. Links a judge account, works through assigned practice sheets, belongs to at most one team, and views their own analytics, the team's combined coverage, and the leaderboard.
  • Mentor: a faculty member or senior who coaches. Everything a student can do, plus authoring practice sheets, assigning them to students, coaching teams, and adding learning resources.

Entities and what the system records about them

  • University. Every user and every team belongs to one university. Leaderboards can be filtered by university.
  • User. Name, unique e-mail, password hash, role, year of study, university, join date, short bio and avatar.
  • Judge account. A user's handle on an online judge (initially Codeforces; the design allows AtCoder later). A user has at most one account per platform, a handle is unique within a platform, and the account carries the judge-side rating and the time the handle was verified. It cannot exist without its owner.
  • Problem. A problem hosted on a judge (identified there by a platform-specific code such as 1234C) or a manually added link. It has a title, a numeric rating, a URL, and a flag for whether an editorial exists. A problem is tagged with any number of topics (Graphs, Dynamic Programming, Flows, …).
  • Contest. A rated round, a past-ICPC set mirrored in the Codeforces Gym, or a practice set. It has a name, kind, start time and duration, and includes an ordered set of problems labelled A, B, C, ….
  • Submission. One attempt by a user at a problem, pulled from the judge by a periodic sync job: verdict, language, timestamp, and the judge's own submission id. A submission made during a contest is linked to that contest; a practice submission is not.
  • Participation. A user's result in a rated contest: old rating, new rating and rank. This is the source of the rating-history chart.
  • Practice sheet. A mentor-authored, ordered list of problems with a level, a description and an optional deadline. A sheet may instead point at a past-ICPC contest, in which case its problems are that contest's problems. Sheets are assigned to students; a student's progress is the set of sheet problems they have solved (detected automatically from submissions) or ticked off by hand (for problems the sync cannot see).
  • Team. An ICPC squad of at most three students from one university, coached by a mentor, with an invite code that lets a student join.
  • Learning resource. An article, video, notes, roadmap, tutorial or book for one topic and level, added by a mentor. Users can bookmark resources.
  • Calendar event. A practice session, team meeting or announcement, either personal or belonging to a team. Users may set a reminder on an event. Contest dates and sheet deadlines also appear on the calendar but are not stored as events, because they are already recorded on the contest and sheet.

Business rules

  1. A user has exactly one role (student or mentor) and belongs to exactly one university.
  2. A user has at most one judge account per platform; a handle is unique on its platform.
  3. A problem is uniquely identified on its platform by its external code; the same problem can appear in several contests (for example a Div. 1 and Div. 2 mirror).
  4. Within a contest every problem has a distinct label (A, B, C …).
  5. Every submission belongs to exactly one user and one problem. Contest submissions reference their contest; practice submissions do not.
  6. A user has at most one participation record per contest.
  7. A sheet has exactly one author, who must be a mentor. The problems in a sheet are ordered, and each position is used once.
  8. A student can be assigned a sheet only once, and can mark complete only problems that belong to a sheet they are assigned.
  9. A team has exactly one coach (a mentor), belongs to one university, and has at most three members. A student is a member of at most one team.
  10. A resource covers exactly one topic and is added by one user.
  11. A reminder belongs to one user and one event; a user has at most one reminder per event.
  12. Counts and statuses that can be computed (problems solved, streak, sheet progress, contest status, difficulty band, leaderboard rank) are never stored; they are derived by queries so that they cannot become stale.

3.3. Relational Schema

The ER model is mapped to relations with the standard rules:

  • Strong entity → one relation with the entity's key as primary key.
  • Weak entity (JudgeAccount) → a relation whose primary key is the owner's key plus the partial key.
  • 1 : N relationship → the key of the "1" side becomes a foreign key on the "N" side (studies_at, based_at, coaches, on_problem, submits, during, authors, archives, covers, adds, schedules). Where participation of the N side is partial the foreign key is nullable (during, archives, schedules).
  • N : 1 relationship with attributes whose N side has at most one instance (belongs_to) → a separate relation keyed by the N side, because a user's membership row is optional and carries its own attributes.
  • M : N relationship → a junction relation whose primary key is the union of the two keys, plus the relationship's attributes (tagged, includes, participates, contains, assigned, bookmarks, reminds).
  • Ternary relationship (completes) → a relation keyed by all three participating keys.
  • Derived attributes (difficulty, status) are not stored; they are views (§3.5).

Primary keys are underlined; foreign keys are marked with → and the relation they reference.

Why the FDs matter. Each non-trivial FD is either a primary-key dependency or a uniqueness rule from §3.1. The second kind (email → user_id, (platform, handle) → user_id, (platform, external_code) → problem_id, (contest_id, label) → problem_id, (sheet_id, position) → problem_id, invite_code → team_id, url → resource_id) tells us each of those relations has more than one candidate key. That is exactly the situation in which 3NF and BCNF can differ, so those relations are checked most carefully in §3.4.4. Dependencies that are deliberately absent also matter: rating does not determine a stored difficulty, and a problem does not determine a contest, because a problem can be shared by two contests (rule 3). Both would otherwise have created redundancy.

3.4.2 Candidate keys by attribute closure

For each relation the candidate keys are the minimal attribute sets whose closure under the FDs is the whole relation. The closures below are computed for the relations where the answer is not immediate.

Problem

R = {problem_id, platform, external_code, title, rating, url, has_editorial}.

  • {problem_id}+ = {problem_id} ∪ {platform, external_code, title, rating, url, has_editorial} = R  → candidate key.
  • {platform, external_code}+ = {platform, external_code, problem_id} → then apply problem_id → … = R  → candidate key.
  • {platform}+ = {platform}; {external_code}+ = {external_code}; {rating}+ = {rating}  → not keys, so both keys above are minimal.

Candidate keys: {problem_id}, {platform, external_code}. Primary key: problem_id (shorter, stable if a judge renames a code). Prime attributes: problem_id, platform, external_code.

JudgeAccount

R = {user_id, platform, handle, rating, verified_at}.

  • {user_id, platform}+ = {user_id, platform, handle, rating, verified_at} = R  → candidate key.
  • {platform, handle}+ = {platform, handle, user_id} → {user_id, platform} → R  → candidate key.
  • {user_id}+ = {user_id}; {handle}+ = {handle}  → neither key is reducible.

Candidate keys: {user_id, platform}, {platform, handle}. Primary key: (user_id, platform), because it is the identifying relationship of the weak entity. All attributes except rating and verified_at are prime.

Submission

R = {submission_id, user_id, problem_id, contest_id, verdict, language, submitted_at, platform, ext_sub_id}.

  • {submission_id}+ = R  → candidate key.
  • {platform, ext_sub_id}+ = {platform, ext_sub_id, submission_id} → R  → candidate key.
  • {user_id, problem_id, submitted_at}+ = {user_id, problem_id, submitted_at}. A user can submit the same problem twice in one second, so this is not a key, which is why the surrogate id is needed.

Candidate keys: {submission_id}, {platform, ext_sub_id}. Primary key: submission_id.

TeamMember

R = {user_id, team_id, member_role, joined_at}.

  • {user_id}+ = {user_id, team_id, member_role, joined_at} = R  → candidate key.
  • {team_id, user_id}+ = R, but it contains the key {user_id}, so it is a superkey, not a candidate key.
  • {team_id}+ = {team_id}; {team_id, member_role}+ = {team_id, member_role}  → not keys (two members of a team may share a role label).

Candidate key: {user_id}. This is why the relation is keyed by user_id alone rather than by (team_id, user_id), which the M : N mapping rule would have produced for a relationship without the "one team per student" rule.

ContestProblem and SheetProblem

Both have the same shape, R = {X, Y, Z} with X,Y → Z and X,Z → Y.

  • {contest_id, problem_id}+ = R; {contest_id, label}+ = {contest_id, label, problem_id} = R.
  • {contest_id}+ = {contest_id}; {problem_id}+ = {problem_id}; {label}+ = {label}.

Candidate keys: {contest_id, problem_id} and {contest_id, label}; likewise {sheet_id, problem_id} and {sheet_id, position}. Every attribute of these relations is prime.

User, Team, Resource, University, Topic, Contest

Each has a surrogate id whose closure is the whole relation, plus one natural key from a uniqueness rule (email; invite_code and (univ_id, name); url; name; name; (platform, external_id)). The closure of each natural key reaches the surrogate id in one step and then the rest of the relation, so each is a second candidate key. The surrogate is chosen as primary key in every case.

All-key and single-key relations

ProblemTopic has only trivial FDs, so its whole heading is the only candidate key. Participation, SheetAssignment, SheetCompletion, Bookmark, Reminder, Sheet and CalendarEvent each have exactly one non-trivial determinant, which is therefore the only candidate key.

3.4.3 Normalization from 1NF to BCNF

The starting point is the way the data was actually kept before this design: the front-end prototype stores everything in a handful of denormalised records, and a coach tracks sheet progress in a spreadsheet with one row per (sheet, student, problem). Taken together they amount to a single universal relation for sheet tracking:

PROGRESS(sheet_id, sheet_title, deadline, mentor_id, mentor_name, mentor_email, student_id, student_name, student_email, univ_id, univ_name, team_id, team_name, coach_id, problem_id, problem_title, platform, external_code, rating, difficulty, topics, completed_at)

  • F2: mentor_id → mentor_name, mentor_email
  • F3: student_id → student_name, student_email, univ_id, team_id
  • F4: univ_id → univ_name
  • F5: team_id → team_name, coach_id

Closure: {sheet_id, student_id, problem_id}+ reaches every attribute through F1–F9, and no proper subset does (sheet_id alone never reaches student attributes, and so on). So the candidate key is (sheet_id, student_id, problem_id) and the prime attributes are exactly those three, plus platform and external_code, which can substitute for problem_id via F7.

Unnormalised → 1NF

Violation. topics is multivalued: one cell holds a set. 1NF requires every attribute value to be atomic.

Fix. Move the multivalued attribute into its own relation keyed by the problem and the topic. Repeating the PROGRESS row once per topic would also give 1NF but would multiply every other column by the number of topics, so the split is preferred.

1NF → 2NF

Violation. 2NF forbids a non-prime attribute depending on only part of a candidate key. F1, F3 and F6 are exactly such partial dependencies: sheet_title depends on sheet_id alone, student_name on student_id alone, problem_title on problem_id alone. The consequence is visible in the sample: "Graph Theory Foundations" and "Aarav Mehta" are repeated on every row of the sheet, and changing a sheet's deadline means updating one row per assigned student per problem (update anomaly). A sheet with no student assigned yet cannot be recorded at all (insertion anomaly).

Fix. Project each partial dependency into its own relation, keeping only the full-key dependency F9 in the original.

COMPLETION is now all-key plus one attribute that depends on the whole key, so it is in 2NF. Note that COMPLETION still records assignment only implicitly: the row with completed_at = null is what says Saharsh is assigned problem p-042. That is resolved in the 3NF step below.

2NF → 3NF

Violation. 3NF forbids a non-prime attribute depending transitively on the key through another non-prime attribute. Three remain:

  • In SHEET2: sheet_id → mentor_id → mentor_name, mentor_email (F2). A mentor's name is stored once per sheet they wrote.
  • In STUDENT2: student_id → univ_id → univ_name (F4) and student_id → team_id → team_name, coach_id (F5). Renaming a team means editing every member's row, and a team with no members yet cannot exist.
  • In PROBLEM2: problem_id → rating → difficulty (F8). difficulty is a pure function of rating; storing it lets the two disagree.

Fix. Project each transitive dependency out, leaving the foreign key behind.

MENTOR and STUDENT3 have the same attributes apart from role, and a mentor can also be a contestant's coach and a team's coach, so the final design merges them into one User relation with a role attribute. The membership attributes are moved out of STUDENT into TeamMember, keyed by user_id, so that a user without a team does not need a null team_id and the membership can carry its own attributes (member_role, joined_at). Finally the null-valued rows of COMPLETION are replaced by an explicit SheetAssignment relation and an explicit SheetProblem relation, so that "assigned" and "in the sheet" are facts in their own right rather than implied by a null.

3NF → BCNF

BCNF requires that for every non-trivial FD X → Y, X is a superkey, including FDs whose right-hand side is a prime attribute, which 3NF lets through. The relations with more than one candidate key are the ones to check.

A genuine BCNF violation in the first draft of the assignment roster. The mentor roster page was first modelled directly from the screen, which lists, per student, the sheet they are on and the mentor who set it:

ROSTER(student_id, sheet_id, mentor_id)

with the draft rule that a mentor keeps one sheet open per student at a time. The FDs are:

  • G1: sheet_id → mentor_id  (a sheet has one author)
  • G2: student_id, mentor_id → sheet_id  (one open sheet per mentor per student)

Closures: {student_id, sheet_id}+ = R via G1, and {student_id, mentor_id}+ = R via G2 then G1. Both are candidate keys, and they overlap. Every attribute is prime, so the relation is trivially in 3NF. But G1 has determinant sheet_id, which is not a superkey ({sheet_id}+ = {sheet_id, mentor_id} ≠ R). ROSTER is therefore not in BCNF, and it shows the classic symptom: the author of sheet s-01 is repeated once for every student assigned to it, and if the last student is unassigned the sheet's author is lost.

Decomposition on the offending FD, R → (X ∪ Y) and (R − Y):

SHEET_AUTHOR(sheet_id, mentor_id)   → absorbed into Sheet.author_id

SHEET_ASSIGNMENT(student_id, sheet_id)

Both projections are in BCNF (each has a single candidate key that is the determinant of its only FD), the join is lossless because the common attribute sheet_id is a key of SHEET_AUTHOR, and the drafting rule G2 is no longer needed as a dependency: it can be imposed later by a partial unique index if mentors want it, or dropped, which is what the final design does.

Two places where a BCNF violation was avoided by design rather than by decomposition.

  • Submission does not store the contest label of the problem. Had it done so, the FD (contest_id, problem_id) → label would hold inside Submission with a determinant that is not a superkey. The label lives only in ContestProblem.
  • TeamMember does not store coach_id or univ_id. Had it done so, team_id → coach_id would hold with team_id not a superkey. Those attributes live only in Team.

All twenty relations are in BCNF. Every decomposition performed above was on a functional dependency whose left-hand side is a key of one of the resulting relations, so each is lossless, and since every FD in §3.4.1 can be checked within a single relation, the decomposition is also dependency-preserving.

What is deliberately not a table. The prototype's records carried many derived counters: a problem's status and solvedBy, a sheet's total, completed and assigned, a user's solved, streak and maxRating, a contest's status and participants, and the entire leaderboard. Each is a function of the base relations, so storing it would introduce an update anomaly every time a submission arrived. They are implemented as the views in §3.5.
